## Material evidence units

### M1. Quote scratchpad plus contextual examples improved one long-document recall evaluation

**Source:** Anthropic, “[Prompt engineering for Claude’s long context window](https://www.anthropic.com/news/prompting-long-context)” (primary vendor experiment), **Evaluation/Results**.

- **Claim/result:** On the reported best prompt versus baseline, Claude 2 improved from **0.939 to 0.961 accuracy**, a **36% reduction in errors**. Anthropic states that extracting relevant quotes into a scratchpad helped “in all head-to-head comparisons,” with a small latency cost.
- **Model/task:** Claude Instant 1.2 was the primary model. Claude 2 was reported only for baseline and the best Instant-derived strategy. Task was multiple-choice recall from a long, randomized-collage government document.
- **Intervention/comparators:** Base answer-only prompt versus two unrelated general-knowledge examples, two or five examples drawn from other document chunks, each with or without a scratchpad instructing the model to pull relevant quotes.
- **Context and method:** The source reports testing roughly **75,000 and 90,000 tokens** while also describing the experiment as **70K and 95K token documents**. It placed answer material at beginning, middle, or end. Questions were generated by Claude from a July 13 government document, then items Claude missed with only the source passage were excluded. Short-context retained-question accuracy was about **90%**. A random non-source section yielded **34%** accuracy versus **25%** four-choice chance.
- **Caveats:** The published charts contain additional results but do not expose their values as text. The evaluation is vendor-authored, synthetic multiple-choice recall, one source corpus, and excludes difficult items. Some generated “correct” answers were actually wrong. This is evidence for document-QA recall, not production writing, open-ended reasoning, or tool use.
- **Counterevidence within source:** Performance at the **end** of the document “can be degraded” by scratchpad/examples, plausibly because examples increase the distance from end evidence to the answer. Unrelated examples did not appear to help.
- **Decision dimension:** Use quote extraction and in-context examples when document-grounded recall is the measured bottleneck. Measure position sensitivity and latency before rollout.
- **Source-unique:** Yes.

### M2. Long-context example selection often does not beat random selection

**Source:** Baek, Lee, and Gupta, “[Revisiting In-Context Learning with Long Context Language Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16926),” Sections 2.2–2.3 and Table 1.

- **Claim/result:** Across **18 datasets** and four task families, sophisticated relevance, diversity, curriculum, and hard-example selection were statistically significant over random selection in **fewer than 15%** of experiment instances. The study reports that some selection methods sometimes performed worse. Simple augmentation of demonstrations improved performance by **5%** in certain low-resource datasets.
- **Models/tasks:** Gemini 1.5 Flash (**1M-token** context), Gemini 1.5 Pro (**2M tokens**), and Llama 3.1 70B (**128K tokens**). Tasks: translation, summarization, BBH multiple-choice reasoning, and classification.
- **Method/comparator:** Random fixed demonstrations versus query-relevance, diversity, curriculum, and hard-example selection. Demonstration counts doubled from 1 until data or context capacity was exhausted. Translation and summarization used **3 runs** per setup. Reasoning and classification used **10 runs**. Significance used a t-test at **95% confidence**.
- **Cost/computation:** Query-conditioned relevance selection is reported as \(O(n^2)\) in demonstration-token length, versus \(O(kn)\) for reusable fixed random demonstrations, where \(n \gg k\). The latter can use KV caching.
- **Caveats:** The result concerns many-shot ICL selection, not quote extraction. Performance rose initially but could decline when contexts became heavily populated. The authors attribute this to long-context processing difficulty. Exact dollar/token billing is not reported.
- **Decision dimension:** Do not pay per-query retrieval/selection overhead merely because it is sophisticated. Compare it to a fixed, cacheable random or curated contextual set on the target workload.
- **Source-unique:** Yes.

### M3. External or structured feedback can improve code repair, but feedback quality and iteration count matter

**Source:** “[FeedbackEval: A Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models in Feedback-Driven Code Repair Tasks](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06939),” Abstract, Sections 5–7, Tables 3–4.

- **Claim/result:** Across the study’s conditions, **mixed feedback** achieved **63.6%** repair success, LLM-Expert **62.9%**, test feedback **57.9%**, minimal feedback **53.1%**, compiler feedback **49.2%**, and LLM-Skilled feedback **48.8%**. The authors report diminishing marginal benefit after **two or three iterations**.
- **Models/tasks:** GPT-4o, Claude-3.5, DeepSeek-R1, GLM-4, and Qwen2.5. Code repair benchmark assembled from HumanEval, CoderEval, and SWE-Bench-verified. Error instances came from rule-based mutation, LLM-induced perturbation, and naturally incorrect LLM outputs.
- **Intervention/comparators:** Six feedback modalities, including static-analysis/compiler and test signals, LLM-simulated human-like signals, and composite feedback. Repair@1 compares feedback types. Repair@3 regenerates feedback after each remaining error for three iterations.
- **Quoted/located support:** The abstract reports: “mixed feedback yields the highest repair success (63.6%)” and “LLM-Skilled proves least effective (48.8%).” Section 6 specifies “three iterations” and Repair@3.
- **Caveats:** These are aggregate benchmark outcomes, not a feedback-free baseline. “LLM-Expert” includes ground-truth knowledge, so it is not a deployable black-box verifier in most production settings. The paper acknowledges potential training-data leakage and uses identical prompts/hyperparameters across models. Its task is code repair, not general text or reasoning.
- **Decision dimension:** Prefer test/compiler/verifier diagnostics tied to observed failure over ungrounded critique. Cap iterations and require revalidation after every repair.
- **Source-unique:** Yes.

### M4. Self-generated verification and repair can degrade reasoning and planning output

**Source:** Stechly, Valmeekam, and Kambhampati, “[On the Self-Verification Limitations of Large Language Models on Reasoning and Planning Tasks](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08115v2),” Abstract.

- **Claim/result:** The authors report that, “Across almost all of our domains, this self-verification system worsens performance,” and that more backprompts “consistently degrades output quality.”
- **Model/method/comparator:** GPT-4 generated an initial solution, then acted as its own verifier/critic. Its critique was included in a backprompt for another generation. This iterative LLM+LLM loop is compared with non-self-corrected generation across the study’s reasoning and planning domains.
- **Mechanism/caveat:** The authors attribute degradation to a substantial verifier false-negative rate: valid solutions are often rejected, causing damaging repair. This is direct counterevidence to feedback-free or same-model self-critique as a default repair mechanism. It does not establish that a high-quality external executable verifier will fail.
- **Exact limitation:** The inspected abstract passage does not provide domain-level numerical deltas in accessible text. Do not convert the qualitative finding into a percentage.
- **Decision dimension:** Acceptance must remain controlled by an independently calibrated or executable verifier. Preserve an accepted candidate unless the verifier provides actionable, reliable failure evidence.
- **Source-unique:** Yes.

## Operational decision table

| Situation | Choose | Comparator and stopping rule | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long document QA with evidence buried away from prompt end | Ask for relevant verbatim quotes before final answer. Add document-grounded examples. | Base prompt versus quote scratchpad, at 70K/95K-equivalent lengths and beginning/middle/end evidence placement. | End-position accuracy falls or latency violates budget. |
| Many-shot contextual examples | Start with a fixed, cacheable curated/random set. | Compare against query-selected examples with equal token budget. | No statistically credible gain after accounting for retrieval and cache costs. |
| Code/tool repair with executable tests or validators | Feed failing tests, compiler/static diagnostics, and failure location into repair. Re-run verifier after each patch. | One repair versus 2–3 repair iterations, with true end-state validation. | Acceptance rises while independent validity does not, or marginal gain is exhausted. |
| Same-model “critique then rewrite” without an external check | Do not deploy as an automatic repair default. | Compare against retaining the original answer and against external-verifier feedback. | Verifier rejects known-good candidates or additional loops reduce task success. |

## Counterevidence and transfer limits

- **C1:** Long-context prompting can harm recall when evidence is at the document end. Anthropic’s improvement is not position-invariant.
- **C2:** More contextual examples are not universally better. In many-shot ICL, carefully selected examples rarely beat random selection reliably, and overly full contexts can reduce performance.
- **C3:** Feedback is not inherently corrective. GPT-4 self-verification/backprompting degraded performance across almost all studied reasoning/planning domains.
- **C4:** FeedbackEval’s numerical gains apply to code repair and mixed feedback. They do not demonstrate improvement for prose, general reasoning, or tools without a reliable external verifier.
- **C5:** No inspected primary source reports a production dollar-cost comparison for quote scratchpads or repair loops. Input/output tokens, calls, latency, and verified-success-per-dollar need local measurement.
