# T2 Holdout: Counterevidence and Operational-Inference Note

**As-of cutoff:** 2026-09-07  
**Assigned question:** R2, reliability limits, evaluator validity, leakage, nulls, and the evidence needed to design fixed-model production experiments for run-design changes.  
**Boundary:** This is not a survey or ranking of context, tool-feedback, repair, retry, or decomposition interventions. It does not recommend a model change, claim universal rankings, or make security claims.

## Bounded answer

Run-design effects must be measured as distributions of outcomes under a fixed model, reasoning effort, task set, evaluator, and resource policy, not as a single benchmark score. The strongest constraints are:

- **Evaluator validity:** passing issue-specific tests is not equivalent to a correct patch. On SWE-bench Verified patches that initially passed, an audit found missed regressions, divergent behavior, and substantial ambiguity.
- **Run variance:** even nominally identical coding-agent configurations vary materially across repeated runs, including at temperature 0. Small single-run gains are not decision-grade.
- **Benchmark transfer and leakage:** static public tasks can overstate capability or scaffold fit. Fresh tasks reduce but do not eliminate contamination and remain test-oracle limited.
- **Cost must remain visible:** validation, retries, and differential testing can improve or alter selection signals while consuming materially different runtime, tokens, tool calls, and test compute.

Therefore a production change should be treated as an **adoption candidate only after a pre-registered paired fixed-model experiment** shows a repeatable benefit on locked and fresh holdouts, without a material evaluator-integrity or cost regression. The specific thresholds below are **proposed local controls**, not source-established universal gates.

## Findings

### F1. A passing benchmark patch can be incorrect

[Wang, Pradel, and Liu, *Are “Solved Issues” in SWE-bench Really Solved Correctly?*, ICSE 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2) audited one plausible patch per task from CodeStory, LearnByInteract, and OpenHands on the 500-task SWE-bench Verified set. Standard SWE-bench validation runs only PR-modified test files.

**Method and comparator.** The authors re-ran *all* available developer tests against the same initially passing patches. They then used PatchDiff, which uses an oracle patch plus LLM-generated differentiating tests, and manually examined a 30% sample of the resulting suspicious patches.

| Measured contrast | Outcome | Qualification |
|---|---:|---|
| Standard issue-test evaluator vs all developer tests | 7.8% of plausible patches failed additional developer tests. Mean resolved-rate reduction: **4.5 pp** | CodeStory: 62.2% to 57.0% (-5.2 pp), LearnByInteract: 60.2% to 55.6% (-4.6 pp), OpenHands: 53.0% to 49.2% (-3.8 pp). Non-functional convention-only failures were excluded. |
| Plausible patch vs oracle-patch behavior under PatchDiff | **260/877 (29.6%)** were behaviorally divergent | “Suspicious” is not synonymous with incorrect. Of 77 manually assessed suspicious patches, 51 (66.2%) remained uncertain because requirements were under-specified. |
| Manual sample extrapolation | Estimated **11.0%** of plausible patches incorrect, implying **6.4 pp** mean resolved-rate inflation | This is an extrapolation from a 77-patch sample, assumes its incorrectness rate represents all suspicious patches, and is limited to PatchDiff-detectable divergences. |

PatchDiff’s cost was reported as **$0.105 per patch, $91.716 total** at two repair iterations, detecting 260/877 suspicious patches. With zero repair iterations, it detected 228/877 at **$0.039 per patch, $33.962 total**. These figures are specific to its GPT-4o-mini test-generation setup and historical API prices, not a production cost forecast.

**Operational implication.** A run-design change that raises “resolved” rate on a limited hidden-test evaluator has not thereby demonstrated better software outcomes. Report at least: task-test pass, broader regression-test pass where feasible, patch-application rate, evaluator/parser failures, and a reviewed sample of wins and regressions.

### F2. Validation mechanisms are themselves conditional and can be expensive

The PatchDiff audit’s method is useful evidence of an evaluator limitation, but it cannot serve unchanged as a production acceptance oracle:

- It needs the developer oracle patch and test patch, which a production agent normally does not have.
- Its differentiating-test generation used GPT-4o-mini at temperature 1, so its detection coverage and cost are method-specific.
- Its manual correctness assessment involved two authors resolving ambiguous cases, and only 77 suspicious patches were examined. The reported 95% confidence interval for that sample was 9.39%.
- It only studies patches emitted by three agents on Python SWE-bench Verified. It does not establish the same false-positive rate for another agent, language, task mix, or evaluator.

**Proposed control:** Use differential tests only as an offline **audit layer** when an independent reference implementation or accepted patch exists. Do not call a candidate incorrect merely because it differs from a reference patch. Treat a confirmed behavioral regression as a failure, and an unconfirmed divergence as a review item.

### F3. One-run agent scores are too noisy for small run-design effects

[Bijarnason, Silva, and Monperrus, *On Randomness in Agentic Evals* (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150) executed ten independent evaluations for each configuration on SWE-bench Verified. Its study covered three models, two scaffolds, six configurations, two temperature conditions, **60,000 trajectories**, 25.58B tokens, and 1.88M tool calls.

**Measured reliability limits:**

- Single-run pass@1 ranged **2.2–6.0 pp** across repeat evaluations of an identical configuration. Standard deviations exceeded **1.5 pp**, including at temperature 0.
- At temperature 0, examples include DeepSWE-preview/nano-agent: **20.4 ± 1.0%**, range 18.2–21.4%; Qwen3-32B/R2E-Gym: **22.3 ± 1.8%**, range 19.8–25.2%.
- The study attributes persistence at temperature 0 to inference and environment non-determinism. It observed trajectory divergence within the first 1% of the trajectory.
- The authors’ power analysis states that, at its median variance, detecting a 1 pp improvement requires **36 runs per agent** for 80% power. This is a study-specific calculation, not a universal requirement.
- The gap between optimistic `pass@5` and pessimistic `pass^5` reached **24.9 pp**, demonstrating that retries alter the operating point rather than merely yielding a more precise pass@1 estimate.

**Operational implication.** Do not treat “temperature 0” as deterministic, or a best retry as a pass@1 result. A retry policy must be evaluated as the actual deployed policy and reported separately from single-attempt performance.

### F4. Static benchmark results need fresh-transfer checks

[SWE-bench’s original paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.06770) defines success as applying a patch and passing the task’s fail-to-pass and pass-to-pass tests. Its own training setup used repository-disjoint training data “to eliminate the risk of data contamination,” and it proposed later-created tasks for temporal robustness. That design choice supports the concern but does not prove that any particular released model is contaminated.

[SWE-bench-Live](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23419) provides a useful transfer warning. It constructed 1,319 Python issue-resolution tasks from 93 repositories, with issues created January 2024–April 2025, reproducible containers, and repeated task-validation runs retaining only consistent test transitions. The authors report that, under their stated settings, OpenHands + Claude 3.7 Sonnet reached **43.20%** on SWE-bench Verified but the highest rate on SWE-bench-Live was **19.25%**. This is evidence that a static benchmark result does not transfer automatically to newer tasks. It is **not** proof that the entire difference is leakage, nor a model-independent benchmark ranking.

The original SWE-bench paper also found that its fine-tuned SWE-Llama models, trained with oracle retrieval context, performed poorly with BM25 retrieval. This is direct counterevidence to any assumption that a context change is uniformly beneficial. It supports measuring the exact deployment context distribution.

## Contradictions and transfer limits

| Limit | What it constrains |
|---|---|
| Test passing is incomplete | A hidden-test score measures the supplied test oracle, not all intended behavior, maintainability, or security. |
| Differential behavior is incomplete evidence | PatchDiff finds divergence, not correctness. Its “6.4 pp inflation” estimate depends on sampled manual classification and representativeness assumptions. |
| Freshness is not proof of absence of leakage | Later task dates reduce exposure risk but do not establish a model’s training contents or eliminate public issue/PR accessibility. |
| “Same configuration” is not necessarily reproducible | Provider serving, hardware, batching, tools, environment timing, and test flakiness can vary even at temperature 0. |
| Benchmark transfer is task-distribution dependent | SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench-Live differ in repositories, dates, task construction, environments, and difficulty. Their score gap cannot identify a single cause. |
| Retry results are a different product policy | `pass@k`, best-of-k, and single-attempt pass@1 answer different questions and have different cost and latency. |
| No security conclusion | None of the retained studies measures production security impact for run-design changes. |

## Proposed fixed-model paired evaluation

The following is a **proposed local experiment design**, derived from the constraints above.

### Design

1. **Freeze invariants.** Pin model identifier and provider revision if available, reasoning-effort setting, system prompt except the intervention, tool permissions, repo base commit, environment image, evaluator version, task list, wall-clock cap, and token/tool-call budgets. Record items that cannot be pinned.
2. **Define one intervention.** Example: baseline context selection versus candidate context selection. Do not change retries, validation policy, decomposition, tool descriptions, model, or reasoning effort in the same comparison.
3. **Use paired runs.** Execute baseline and candidate on every task and replicate. Randomize A/B order per `(task, replicate)` to reduce order and infrastructure effects.
4. **Pre-register outcomes.** Primary: independently evaluated task success. Secondary: broader regression outcome, patch-apply rate, median and tail latency, token cost, tool calls, test executions, stop reason, and human-review findings.
5. **Separate tuning from decision.** Tune the candidate only on a development set. Lock it before the holdout. Include both a static comparison set and a newer local holdout whose issue/PR is after the fixed model’s documented cutoff where that information is available.
6. **Run a variance pilot.** Use repeated identical baseline runs to estimate local variance and size the holdout replication count by power analysis. Do not borrow a universal run count from another model/scaffold.
7. **Audit evaluator integrity.** Keep evaluator code and hidden tests outside the writable workspace. Re-run winning patches against the full available suite where feasible. Sample discordant and high-impact patches for review.

## Proposed operational decision table

| Signal | Proposed interpretation | Proposed action |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate wins under locked evaluator, full-suite audit, and fresh holdout, with planned paired analysis supporting a positive effect | Evidence for the measured mechanism in the evaluated task/resource regime | Advance to a limited production trial with the same model and resource policy. |
| Candidate improves task-test pass but loses full-suite checks, has evaluator-integrity uncertainty, or introduces confirmed behavioral regressions | Benchmark gain is not adequate evidence of production improvement | Do not adopt. Diagnose evaluator or patch-quality failure. |
| Mean improvement is below the locally powered detectable effect, confidence interval crosses zero, or one-run result is the only evidence | Inconclusive, not a null finding | Increase planned replication only if the expected business value justifies its cost. |
| Candidate improves `pass@k`/best-of-k but not single-attempt pass@1, or materially raises p95 latency/cost | It changes retry economics, not necessarily first-pass capability | Evaluate and decide as a retry-policy change with explicit success, latency, and cost constraints. |
| Static benchmark gain does not reproduce on newer/local tasks | Limited transfer evidence | Keep the intervention experimental. Do not extrapolate from the static benchmark. |
| Context intervention loses or becomes unstable on task strata unlike its tuning distribution | Context-distribution shift is a credible failure mode | Restrict scope to demonstrated strata or redesign and retest. |

## Remaining gaps and stop reason

1. **No production-specific task distribution or cost envelope was supplied.** Therefore no justified numerical adoption threshold, sample size, latency limit, or cost ceiling can be specified.
2. **No model cutoff, provider determinism guarantee, or evaluator architecture was supplied.** Leakage controls and reproducibility controls must be instantiated locally.
3. **No source here measures security effects of these run-design choices.** Security must be evaluated separately rather than inferred from test success.
4. **The reviewed evidence is concentrated on Python repository issue resolution and SWE-bench-family evaluators.** It does not establish results for other languages, interactive product tasks, or proprietary repositories.

Research stopped after the primary sources converged on the decision-changing constraints: incomplete test oracles, repeated-run variance, static-to-fresh transfer risk, and context-distribution sensitivity.
